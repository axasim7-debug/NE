V. Operational/Security Factors applied to reach the Preliminary Opinion

Often inmates demonstrate immature, dysfunctional, and combative behaviors if there is an
inference, subjective impression, and/or perception that the inmate thinks that they are not being
treated in a professional, truthful, desirable, and/or fair manner by authorities. The confined
inmate, if given even this perception that he/she is being mistreated by (examples) family

                                I. Background and Qualifications

1.     I am a Professor in the Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences at Texas A&M

University (TAMU). I am also formerly the Director of Clinical Training (2012-2016) of the

doctoral training program in Clinical Psychology at TAMU. Additionally, I was a licensed

psychologist in the state of Texas for 20 years until I retired my license (in good standing) in

2019. Since 1998, I have been actively involved in education and training in the areas of

psychological and personality assessment, forensic psychology, violence risk assessment,

abnormal psychology, research methodology, and professional ethics. In addition to classroom

teaching, I have been invited to conduct advanced training workshops for mental health, legal,

and criminal justice professionals throughout North America, Europe, Asia, and Australia.

2.     I have conducted research on psychological assessment and diagnosis and the prediction

of human behavior since the 1990s and have published approximately 200 peer-reviewed

scientific journal articles, book chapters, and professional manuals related to these topics. Most

of my research has focused on forensic and correctional mental health assessment issues, such as

other forms of socially deviant behavior inside and outside institutional settings.1

3.     In addition to conducting basic research on psychological assessment, I have published

extensively on controversies concerning the use of various psychiatric diagnoses, psychological

tests, and assessment instruments and procedures in forensic and correctional settings,




                                                   1
particularly those intended to assess psychopathic and antisocial personality traits such as the

Hare Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) and the American Psychiatric Association’s

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) diagnosis of antisocial

personality disorder (ASPD).2 I have also consulted on these and related mental health issues in

approximately 50 capital murder trials over the course of my career.

                              II. Referral Question & Background

4.     I have been asked by defense counsel for Bryan Kohberger to briefly summarize the

relevant literature concerning the scientific status of potential expert testimony that a criminal

defendant involved in capital murder litigation is a “psychopath,” “sociopath,” or has ASPD.

This declaration was prepared in anticipation that the prosecution in this case may attempt to

administer the Hare PCL-R (or other similar diagnostic interviews) in their pre-trial mental

health evaluations of Mr. Kohberger and/or attempt to introduce evidence from mental health

expert witnesses at trial to label him with these pejorative terms.

5.     I note at the outset that I have not conducted an independent psychological evaluation of

Mr. Kohberger. Nor would I ever provide a rating for him on an instrument such as the PCL-R as

part of legal proceedings, based on the problems with this rating scale that I detail below (see

“Relevant Scientific and Professional Literature” section concerning the poor reliability and

predictive validity of the PCL-R). I have no opinion as to what if any psychiatric diagnoses Mr.

Kohberger may (or may not) experience currently or at earlier points in his life, as well as no

opinion as to their potential relevance in regard to explaining his alleged criminal conduct. The

fact that I have not evaluated Mr. Kohberger has no bearing on the opinions that I provide in

relation to this case, as these opinions are specific to the scientific validity of psychopathy and

ASPD evidence in capital murder trials.




                                                  2
                       III. Relevant Scientific and Professional Literature

6.      It is unfortunately quite common for forensic mental health expert opinions in capital

murder cases to be based on methods and instruments that are poorly suited to reliably and

validly answer the various referral questions (e.g., mental state, future dangerousness, intellectual

disability) that examiners may be asked to address.3 In the context of potential psychopathy

evidence being presented in relation to Mr. Kohberger, there are significant problems with the

opinions that might be offered regarding the relevance, as well as probative and prejudicial

value, of psychopathy and ASPD. These problems are described in detail in subsequent

paragraphs.

7.      One of the most direct examples of scientific inadequacy in psychiatric and psychological

evaluations is the fact that practicing mental health professionals evaluating the same individual

frequently fail to agree with each other as to whether that individual is in fact diagnosable as

having any particular disorder, especially personality disorders.4 For example, the field trials

examining inter-rater reliability for the diagnostic criteria in the fifth edition of the DSM

(inclusive of the diagnoses in DSM-5 and its more recent text revision, DSM-5-TR) indicated

that ASPD diagnoses had poor rates of agreement across diagnosticians.5 Given the historically

poor inter-rater reliability of personality disorders such as ASPD noted earlier, numerous

attempts have been made over the years to improve the accuracy of this category of diagnoses.

One method for assessing antisocial and psychopathic traits that initially demonstrated some

greater degree of inter-rater reliability across trained raters in early research publications is the

aforementioned PCL-R, which was described, prematurely, as “reliable and valid” in various

scientific journal articles during the 1990s and early 2000s.




                                                   3
8.     The PCL-R is a 20-item rating scale that is intended to be used by trained mental health

professionals to assess for the presence of the personality disorder of psychopathy. The 20 items

consist of prototypically psychopathic traits (e.g., remorselessness, grandiosity, superficial

charm) but also include items that focus on a history of antisocial and criminal acts (e.g., juvenile

delinquency, past revocation of conditional release). The PCL-R typically is scored based on a

semi-structured interview and review of available collateral information (e.g., institutional files,

past mental health evaluations). Examinees can be given a PCL-R score ranging from 0 (zero) to

40, with higher scores indicating that they are being rated by an examiner as more psychopathic.

Historically, a score of 30 and above has been used as a quasi-diagnostic cut-off for identifying

examinees as being “psychopathic” in North American settings.

The PCL-R is not part of or included in any formalized psychiatric diagnostic system, such as the

American Psychiatric Association’s aforementioned Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental

Disorders (DSM-5). Instead, it is a freestanding rating scale commercially marketed by a

psychological testing company, Multi-Health Systems, Inc. Research indicates that scores from

this rating scale do, however, correlate relatively highly with DSM diagnoses of ASPD,6 which

is to be expected given that the diagnostic criteria for ASPD overlap with several of the items

included within the PCL-R. Compared to the criteria for an ASPD diagnosis, however, the PCL-

R includes several more items on which examiners attempt to rate an examinee’s ostensible

personality characteristics, such as superficial charm, grandiosity, and shallow affect (emotions)

– which are not included in the seven criteria for ASPD.

9.     Noted in the preceding paragraphs, the PCL-R was described as reliable and valid in

numerous early publications. However, since the late 2000s, it is important to point out that

numerous studies have investigated the reliability of PCL-R scores in contested legal




                                                  4
proceedings and have found it to be highly unreliable in this context.7 Levels of agreement in

these more recent “field studies” (i.e., studies investigating reliability in real-world psychological

evaluations rather than in controlled scientific research) have been well below accepted standards

of what would constitute minimal reliability for forensic mental health practice.8

10.    There is recent evidence that even those trained by the PCL-R instrument’s developer,

Robert Hare, through his Darkstone Research Group workshops, struggle to reliably assess the

personality traits included in the PCL-R. Blais, Forth, and Hare (2017)9 summarized reliability

statistics for 280 participants in this training program who went on to score a series of practice

cases that were then evaluated for diagnostic accuracy. The effects of this formalized training

program on inter-rater reliability were disappointing. In particular, the reliability of the

‘personality’ items on the PCL-R was quite poor, indicating a large degree of variability in rating

subjective traits such as remorselessness, superficial charm, and lack of empathy. Notably, these

poor results led the study authors to conclude that those raters’ PCL-R scores “did not meet the

standard recommended for criminal cases” (see “Public Significance Statement,” p. 762). That

is, the evaluation results these individuals produced following this training were so inconsistent

across examiners that they fell below standards for acceptable professional practice in the

criminal justice system.

11.    Even if PCL-R scores could be reliably produced in adversarial legal cases, there are

additional reasons to question their relevance and probative value in capital murder trials. For

example, mock jury research has shown that individuals who believe a defendant is highly

psychopathic also believe that such a defendant will be highly dangerous in the future.10 Despite

this intuitive association between psychopathy and violence, at present there is little evidence to

support the assertion that psychopathy diagnoses have any bearing on a convicted capital



                                                  5
defendant’s potential for future violent acts. That is, the available scientific studies suggest that

psychopathy diagnoses are at best very weakly related to violent behavior in U.S. prisons.11

Therefore, claims that an inmate is more likely to be violent in the future if serving out a life

sentence because that inmate has been judged by a mental health professional to be

“psychopathic” are based on almost no scientific support, and, actually ignore what are known to

be legitimate correlates of future violence in prison settings (e.g., limited education, prison gang

membership).12

12.    In addition to being scientifically unreliable in the context of capital murder evaluations,

the introduction of evidence concerning the PCL-R raises other problems and ethical questions.

Foremost, the term “psychopath” and the traits typically associated with the PCL-R (e.g.,

callousness, grandiosity, superficial charm) have a strong likelihood of unduly prejudicing jurors

against a defendant. Among venirepersons, the psychopath label evokes images of real-world

serial killers such as Ted Bundy, as well as fictional villains such as Hannibal Lecter.13 In fact,

among a sample of over 400 venirepersons participating in a 2013 survey in Dallas County,

Texas, Charles Manson was the most common response (20%) when participants were asked to

spontaneously identify the person they first thought of when hearing the term “psychopath”

(followed by Jeffrey Dahmer [14%], Adolf Hitler [12%], and Ted Bundy [11%]).14

13.    Numerous other published research findings confirm the pejorative effect of believing a

criminal defendant as a psychopath.15 For example, mock jurors in trial simulations who believe

a defendant to be highly psychopathic are more likely to support death verdicts (in capital

murder trials) and more likely to recommend longer criminal sentences (in non-capital trials)

than are participants who believe a defendant to be less psychopathic. Additionally, they are

more likely to rate a defendant as more dangerous and more “evil” than are participants who




                                                   6
believe a defendant to be less psychopathic. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis16 was published

that accumulated all of the scientific literature examining the effects of psychopathy evidence in

jury simulations, with the results indicating that the presentation of such information to mock

jurors resulted in much more punitive outcomes for the defendant when compared to cases in

which psychopathy evidence was not presented.

                                        IV. Opinion

14.    To the extent that the admissibility of expert mental health evidence is to be informed by

scientific data, the above-noted findings strongly argue against the introduction of ASPD and the

PCL-R in cases such as that of Mr. Kohberger. ASPD and PCL-R psychopathy evidence

provided by examiners in adversarial legal settings is highly unreliable, largely irrelevant to the

likelihood of future violent acts in capital cases, and has the strong potential to stigmatize

defendants with an immaterial and pejorative label and associated set of personality traits (e.g.,

grandiose, conning/manipulative).

15.    Please feel free to contact me if you require any further information regarding the issues

raised in this declaration. If needed, I would be happy to describe in greater detail my

reservations about the scientific adequacy of testimony concerning ASPD and the PCL-R in

capital cases.

an array of settings (e.g., pre-adjudication, post-adjudication) and in relation to various referral questions
(e.g., institutional adjustment, response style, presence or absence of mental disorder, treatment outcome).
Issues of legal admissibility are addressed and case examples are reviewed as well.

Psychopathy in the Courtroom: Current Controversies
This intermediate-advanced workshop (4 hours – 8 hours) considers controversies surrounding
instruments designed to measure psychopathic traits among adults and children (e.g., PCL-R, PCL: YV)
and assumes familiarity with commonly used measures. Topics include short- and long-term stability of
psychopathy scores, rater agreement in adversarial contexts, and predictive validity for "bad outcomes"
(e.g., recidivism). Admissibility issues (e.g., relevance, probative & prejudicial value) and associated
legal and ethical issues are also discussed.
