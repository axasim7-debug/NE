          Law enforcement crime scene teams must deal with practical resource limitations.

The team needs room in the evidence vault to store the evidence and manpower to process the

evidence. Given these practical limitations, not every item at a crime scene is collected or

tested.

          Decisions about what areas of a crime scene to process and how to process the crime

scene are based on the skill and experience of the crime-scene response team, the policy or

policies governing the team, any information known about the crime scene, and the facts

presented at the time the crime scene is being processed. This decision-making process also

involves an analysis of the potential probative value of collecting and/or testing a particular

item or portion of the crime scene.

          The first steps to processing a crime scene are assess, observe, and document. This

includes visually observing the crime scene and deciding the extent to which each portion of

the crime scene should be processed. Where, as here, the crime scene is a house, the crime-

scene response team is evaluating which portions of the house were involved in the criminal

activity being investigated. For a homicide, the team is looking for disturbed areas of the

home, blood or other DNA, the general area where the decedent or decedents were found, and

any other indications of potential probative evidence.

        The assessment is not done in a matter of seconds or by quickly glancing at the area.

The team is trained on how to visually inspect the crime scene—using a strong white light

when needed—to look for even the smallest drops of blood.

        When the crime scene response team responded to 1122 King Road, there were areas

of the house that clearly contained probative evidence. These areas included the living room,

hallway, west bedroom, and kitchen on the second floor, the east bedroom on the third floor,

and the stairway areas connecting the floors.

        Other areas of the house at 1122 King Road did not have any indications that

probative evidence would be found. There was no blood found in these areas and nothing

about the area itself that suggested the perpetrator or any victim had been in the area in or

around the time of the crime. These areas included:

            •   The first floor of the residence.

            •   The second floor east bedroom.

            •   The third floor west bedroom and bathroom.

        The areas of the crime scene that clearly contained probative evidence (i.e. living

room, hallway, west bedroom, and kitchen on the second floor, the east bedroom on the third

floor, and the stairway areas connecting the floors) were processed by the crime scene team

using a combination of visual inspection, photographs, and/or LizQ imaging.

        Even in the areas of the residence that clearly contained probative evidence, the crime

scene response team did not choose to collect every item that could have been collected.

Decisions about what items to collect were made by balancing the potential for finding

probative evidence with the practical realities of conducting crime scene investigation. For

example, the crime-scene response team decided that collecting the mattresses and bedframes

that were left behind would not have added probative value to the materials that were

collected and the photographs and visual inspections completed, and those items would have

taken a significant number of resources to collect and store.

        The potential probative value of an item does not depend solely on the presence or

absence of blood. Observations made at the crime scene also inform the analysis. For

example, if a decedent is found lying on a pillow with multiple and/or large wounds that

spilled a significant amount of blood, the probative value of the pillow is lower because the

scene indicates that the blood came from the victim. Further, even if the perpetrator spilled a

minor amount of blood that mixed with the significant amount of blood from the decedent,

the realities of laboratory testing mean the perpetrator’s blood would not be detected. In that

instance, the crime-scene response team may decide to collect only the pillowcase and not the

pillow itself.

        After the assessment and observation stages, the forensic team employs chemical

testing to possible bloodstains (typically red/brown spots) to determine if blood is present. In

this case, the forensic team used ortho-tolidine, which is a presumptive test for blood, to test

throughout the house in any location where possible bloodstains were observed (red/brown

type stains). It is presumptive only because it could interact with substances other than blood

(e.g. potato, watermelon, and other fruits and vegetables). Items that test as positive or

inconclusive for blood are collected for further testing at the lab.

        The crime-scene response team may use amido black and leucocrystal violet to

enhance blood. In this case, the team used amido black because it shows up better on some

surfaces than leucocrystal violet. The team applied amido black in areas where they believed

the perpetrator could have walked or touched and left a bloody footprint or fingerprint. For

example, amido black was applied to the floor of the west bedroom on the second floor, the

stairway half-wall between the second and third floors, and the interior and exterior handle

areas of the sliding glass door in the kitchen.

        The crime-scene response team also swabbed for DNA where there were indications

that the perpetrator may have touched a surface. For example, a possible face mark, hand

marks, and finger mark on the exterior of the south kitchen window were all swabbed for

possible DNA, which could be submitted to the lab for testing.

        The forensic team can also blind swab for DNA when there are facts other than

markings that suggest DNA may be present. For example, the forensic team may blind swab

the steering wheel of a stolen car because it would be expected that the perpetrator touched

the steering wheel during the alleged crime. In this case, the crime scene response team did

not blind swab for DNA because it did not have facts to suggest that the perpetrator touched

surfaces or other areas in the house in a manner that would have left DNA. In addition, the

crime scene response team knew that many people frequented the house, which made it less

likely that swabs of commonly touched surfaces, such as door handles, would provide

probative evidence.

        The crime-scene response team did not test the bathroom drains using ortho-tolidine,

amido black, or any other forensic tools because there were no indications that the perpetrator

had cleaned off in those areas. A reaction to ortho-tolidine or amido black in those locations

6.0      Crime Scene Investigation Methodology

  6.1     There is no one right way to process a crime scene. The methods and the order in
          which those methods are employed is determined by the particulars of the scene
          and the experience of the CSIs. The purpose of processing the crime scene is to
          collect as much information and evidence as possible, in as pristine a condition as
          possible. Evidence collection is more than the physical collection of items from the
          scene; cataloging the interrelationships of where items are in the scene, noting the
          physical layout of the scene, and documenting observations of things that cannot
          be physically collected are all integral parts of collecting evidence. Content and
          context of the scene are both important aspects,
      6.2 Crime scene investigations take place in environments that have variable
          conditions. Due to this, equipment and reagents may not perform as expected.
          Should a piece of equipment or a reagent fail, the CSI should attempt to determine
          the cause of the failure and whether the failure will affect scene processing. Notes
          shall be made about the equipment or reagent failure(s). CSIs should notify the
          Crime Scene Coordinator as necessary.
      6.3 There may be extenuating circumstances in which an item from a crime scene
          should be processed in the laboratory instead of in the field, even though it is not
          being processed for a particular discipline in which a laboratory examination
          would normally take place. This may be due to the delicate nature of the item, the
           unusual circumstances present at the crime scene, or some other factor that
          warrants treating the item differently.
           6.3.1 Field services examinations may be conducted in the laboratory with
                  advance notice and preparation. If a circumstance arises that warrants
                   consideration of laboratory processing for field services, the Crime Scene
                   Coordinator should be contacted.
      6.4 At any crime scene, the CSI should proceed through six actions.
           6.4.1    Assessing
                        6.4.1.1      Before any action is taken on the scene, the CSI should
                                     assess the circumstances in order to decide on a proper
                                     course of action. This assessment includes resource
                                     determination, risks, and task/procedure determination.
                                     Assessment is an ongoing process as well. The CSI should
                                     continuously assess the situation and adjust the processing
                                     plan as necessary. Remaining flexible is a critical aspect of
                                     responding to and dealing with the crime scene.
            6.4.2   Observing

       6.4.3.   Documenting
                    6.4.3.1         Documentation includes written recordings of
                                    observations, photography of the scene, and the creation of
                                    sketches and/or diagrams. Documentation should be
                                    undertaken from the least intrusive to the most intrusive.
                                    In this way, documentation of observations should precede
                                    photography; photography should precede sketching and
                                    measuring; detailed photography and reconstruction
                                    documentation should be the last types of documentation
                                    completed. Documentation is the critical component; it is
                                    through documentation that we can properly preserve the
                                    original condition and context of the scene, before any
                                    significant alteration. Documentation shall be ongoing as
                                    searching, collecting, and processing of the scene take
                                    place.
                     6.4.3.2        Items should be documented as to their condition as found
                                    (e.g. shirt inside out, shoes laced, etc.). The condition may
                                    be relevant and should not be altered until documentation
                                    is complete, and ideally, not altered at all.
        6.4.4   Searching
                     6.4.4.1        The nature of any search and associated activity is
                                    intrusive, so it should not be undertaken until
                                    documentation is complete. During searches, items may be
                                    moved to be more thoroughly examined, which alters the
                                    scene. Multiple searches, at varying levels of depth, may be
                                     conducted throughout the scene, as appropriate.
                      6.4.4.2        Crime scene searches should be methodical (i.e. ordered
                                     and systematic). The type of search to be conducted should
                                     be determined based on the nature of the terrain/area
                                     being searched, lighting conditions, environmental
                                     conditions, and the size of the item(s) being searched for (if
                                     applicable).

11.0 Evidence


  11.1 Methods of collection and preservation of evidence vary by type of evidence.
       Collection and preservation methods are listed in each section of this manual that
       addresses specific types of evidence. Types of evidence that don't have their own
       section in this manual shall be addressed in this section.
  11.2 Evidence markers may be used to identify items of evidence within a crime scene.
       The same evidence marker may not be used for multiple items within a scene,
       unless they are already comingled together.
       11.2.1 If there is a pile of clothing on the floor, that pile of clothing may be
              identified using one marker. If there are three items of clothing on the floor,
              not comingled together, three separate markers should be used.
       11.2.2 If there are several cartridges cases that are very close to one another (e.g.
              within one to two inches), such that it would be impractical to use several
               markers, they may be identified using one marker. If several cartridge cases
               are in the same area, but are far enough that separate evidence markers
              can be used, then several markers should be used.
  11.3 Items that normally belong in a pair or set should not be packaged together unless

                    14.9.3.1 Bullets that ultimately ricochet off of a surface can leave a
                               lead-in mark that represents the entry side of a ricochet.
                               Lead-in marks are generated when the projectile first
                               encounters a surface and creates a small mark before
                               burrowing deeper into that surface.
                    14.9.3.2 When painted metallic surfaces (e.g, automobiles) are
                               impacted, visual cues may help identify the bullet's
                               direction of travel. A pinch point represents the entry side
                               of a ricochet on painted metallic surfaces. On some such
                               surfaces, the ricochet may leave "waves" (i.e. cracks in the
                                paint) that recede away from the direction of travel (i.e. like
                               waves following a boat traveling across the water).
                    14.9.3.3 Automobile metal is strong, and tends to bend significantly
                               before allowing a bullet to pass. This bending often results
                               in a hole through an automobile body being much larger
                               than the projectile that caused it.
                    14.9.3.4 Plugs are often mistaken as a piece of fired bullet. Using a
                               magnet, one can discern a piece of car metal (usually steel,
                               which will adhere to a magnet) from a bullet fragment
                                (usually copper or lead, which will not adhere to a magnet).

17.0 Trace Evidence


  17.1 Trace evidence consists of a wide variety of materials, is usually very small, and
       may be a fragment of a larger item.
       17.1.1 Trace materials include, but are not limited to, hairs, fibers, glass
              fragments, metal shavings, wood shavings, paint chips, soil, and vegetation.
       17.1.2 Trace materials may be easily dislodged. Care should be taken to avoid loss,
              contamination, and cross-contamination.
  17.2 A strong white light may be utilized at an oblique angle in order to visualize trace
       evidence. If a particular type of evidence being searched for would react witha
       different wavelength of light, an alternate light source (ALS) may be utilized.
       17.2.1 A flashlight or the white light setting on an ALS are the most common
              sources for a strong white light.
       17.2.2 When An ALS may only be utilized by analysts who have received training
              in the appropriate use of an ALS.
                    17.2.2.1 When using an ALS to observe an item/surface, goggles
                                shall be worn.
                     17.2.2.2   The appropriate goggles should be used, depending on
                                wavelength.
                                   WAVELENGTH               CORRESPONDING FILTER
                                   <400nm                   yellow or clear UV safe
                                   400-450nm                yellow
                                   450-540nm                orange
                                   >540-700nm               red
                     17.2.2.3   Noone wavelength will affect every type of fiber. When
                                searching for fibers, a wide range of wavelengths should be
                                utilized.
                     17.2.2.4   Dark colored hairs may not react in the same way as light
                                colored hairs. An ALS may be effective for searching for
                                light colored hairs, but a strong white light may be
                                preferable when searching for dark colored hairs.
                     17.2.2.5   Permanent eye damage can occur from reflected, refracted,
                                or direct illumination by the ALS to the eye. Extreme care
                                should be taken around highly reflective surfaces. An
                                analyst should never look directly into the light or allow
                                beams to bounce off the surface into their own eyes or
                              another person's eyes.
