Behavioral Observations:

 Context of test            Bryan engaged in direct test administration with me in a
 administration:            conference room in the Ada County Jail in Boise, Idaho.

                            Test administration was conducted across several days for
                            several hours at a time, aside from restroom breaks. Bryan
                            requested specific time blocks in the middle of the day
                            (generally for 1 to 1.5 hours) to reportedly engage in recreation
                            time or calisthenics, as he noted limited opportunity to engage
                            in such behaviors that day if not performed during that specific
                            time period (1.e., he did not want to break his routine). Bryan
                            often discussed his preferred routine and specific time frames
                            in great detail, including frequently referring to "things [he
                            needed] to get done" during the day.

 Gross motor functioning:   Notable for occasional, subtle rocking of his upper torso while
                            seated, often while engaged in a cognitive task or listening to
                            someone else (e.g., his lawyer).

                            Multiple instances of pacing back and forth waiting for
                            correctional officers to retrieve him from the conference room
                            when Bryan abruptly had to use the restroom. During these
                            instances, Bryan communicated his perception that it was very
                            urgent, that the urge to urinate "crept up on [him]," and that he
                            was concerned he "might not make it" while waiting for jail
                            staff. On these occasions, Bryan provided no indication prior
                            to such urgency that he needed to use the restroom.


                            Bryan ambulated independently. Given constraints of the
                            setting, I was not able to observe his gait.

 Fine motor functioning:    Right-hand dominant. Notably, on a fine motor dexterity
                            measure, Bryan moved his whole arm and repositioned his
                            upper body slightly to manipulate the items effectively.

Bryan's parents, Michael and MaryAnn Kohberger, and sisters, Amanda and Melissa Kohberger,
also completed symptom inventories capturing their observations of Bryan in certain areas.

In terms of attention and metacognitive aspects of executive functioning, Bryan's family

subclinical level, patterns within family members' responses suggested that Bryan struggles to
concentrate when something is not interesting to him, and family members reported observations
of problems planning effectively, with procrastination, and with disorganization at times. Mother
reported to me that childhood symptoms of attention and EF issues were significantly amplified
in these areas, though some continue to persist to date.

Behavioral regulatory problems were more consistently reported across respondents, though only
in one instance reported to notable excess on questionnaires. Bryan's mother and sisters reported
that he exhibits a variety of impulsive tendencies at times, such as rushing, saying things without
thinking, talking at the wrong time, talking excessively, not thinking before acting, and difficulty
waiting. During interview, his family described these patterns to be very impactful within their
interactions.

All family members also identified numerous symptoms of cognitive and behavioral inflexibility,
and Melissa reported significantly more problems with flexibility than typical for Bryan's age.
For example, family members endorsed that Bryan exhibits rigid thinking and behavior, has
trouble coping with changes in routine, is inflexible, and struggles to accept alternative solutions
to problems. Such aforementioned patterns are commonly characteristic of the behavioral
features of Autism Spectrum Disorder, discussed more below.


Family members also consistently reported limitations in Bryan's self-awareness and ability to
monitor his own behavior, particularly for impact on others. For example, all family members
acknowledged that Bryan doesn't always notice when his behavior directly upsets others and
does not understand why people are upset. Multiple family members also reported that he often
annoys or upsets others without intending to do so, and these patterns were discussed at length
during interview with family.
