       20.5.1 Once surface finds are properly documented and collected, the site should

       20.5.2 Loose debris (e.g. leaves, twigs, etc.) should be removed by hand and
              examined for any potential evidentiary value. Care should be taken for any
              items that have scene context (e.g. sticks fashioned in a cross formation)
              and to ensure that underlying items of evidence are not disturbed.
       20.5.3 Once loose debris is removed, the ground should be reexamined for any
             objects or items of evidence. Ground plants should be removed by cutting
             them at ground level, unless a forensic botanist requests the root system to
             be retained.
  20.6 Screens should be used to examine all litter, debris, or soil removed from the
       excavation site. This should preclude missing small items such as bullet fragments,
       teeth, small bones, and other small artifacts.
       20.6.1 A methodology should be established prior to excavation so that the
               location from which soil was taken is not lost as screen sifting is
               undertaken. By doing so, the location of any evidence that's discovered can
               be documented properly. Additionally, if soil samples are to be collected,
               their location from the site can be determined.
        20.6.2 A two-level screening effort is recommended. The first screen is usually
               made up of 1/4 inch mesh, while the second is made up of 1/16 inch mesh.
               Double screening will make it more likely that small objects such as hairs,
              fibers, or shot pellets are located and recovered.
  20.7 Actual excavation of the site should be undertaken in levels. General archeological
       protocol dictates that excavations proceed in arbitrary levels following the surface
       contour until an evident stratum (i.e. layer) is located (man-made or natural).
       20.7.1 A stratum may be a layer of some specific type of soil, a layer with some
              form of particular debris, or any level in which an artifact or artifacts
               become exposed.
        20.7.2 Once excavated and mapped, the process continues for each newly
               discovered stratum.
        20.7.3 The primary tools required for excavation include trowels and brushes. Use
               of these hand tools helps to prevent damage to the sides of the grave and
               the evidence contained in the grave.
        20.7.4 Because the sequence of artifacts uncovered is important, dirt should be
               removed in no more than 2 to 4 inch layers. If a layer of a material is less
               than 2 inches (e.g. 1 inch layer of gravel), then less material should be
               removed when excavating that stratum.
        20.7.5 As the grave sides are exposed, they should be examined for evidence of
               tool or footwear marks.

                      "Field Services"; the "Task Type" shall be selected as "Field
                      Services". The Case File should be assigned to the analyst who will
                      be writing the report, unless there is associated evidence (e.g.
                      Digital Media) associated with the Field Services response.
             27.2.2   In the "Scene Personnel" panel, a separate entry shall be made for
                      each CSI, for each response date, In each entry, the total travel
                      time, total time on scene, and number of locations shall be entered.
             27.2.3   In the "General Crime Scene" panel, a separate entry may be made
                      for each response event or location. Only the pertinent fields shall
                      be entered in this panel, based on the processing conducted. If
                      three vehicles were processed at a department's garage, it may
                      make sense to create an entry for each vehicle. If the information
                      can be sufficiently conveyed in one entry, without confusion, then
                           only one entry may suffice.
              27.2.4       In the "Autopsy Services" panel, a separate entry should be made
                           for each decedent. Enter the pertinent fields based on the quantity
                           of each item that was collected at autopsy.
              27.2.5       In the "Subject Exemplars" panel, list the quantity for each type of
                           exemplar collected from a person.
              27.2.6       In the "General Notes" panel, additional information may be added
                           to the notes for the case.
                  27.2.7   In the "Report" panel, the case narrative is entered. There is a limit
                           on the number of available characters per entry, so multiple
                           entries may have to be made in order to write a long report.

    1.   Defense Claim: Flaws in Drive Testing

         The defense alleges drive test results don't show actual coverage or show coverage where

no measurement is taken. These allegations made by defense appear to take issue with the


interpolation of the drive test data. In other words, the way that the results of the drive test are

plotted on a map. The FBI Cellular Analysis Survey Team (CAST) conducts drive testing using a

Gladiator Autonomous Receiver (GAR) developed by Gladiator Forensics. The GAR collects the

data, which is then analyzed by Gladiator Forensics software. The software creates the coverage

maps seen on several pages of the report. The coverage maps are created using the actual

measurements taken by the GAR and interpolation using the natural neighbor algorithm. In other

words, it's looking at known values at known locations to determine the expected value at a

location where a measurement couldn't have been taken. The natural neighbor algorithm was

        Regarding analysis of AT&T data records, there is a difference in how analysis is

interpreted depending on the type of record being analyzed. Specifically, FBI CAST examines

each transaction in the raw records to determine reliability, based on several factors. As an


example, when an AT&T data session is initiated on AT&T's network that is not following a

timeout of a previous data session, AT&T records the first cell site and then the subsequent cell

sites associated with the session if applicable. The first cell site recorded in this session is the


only cell site that is chosen based on measurements made by the cellphone representing the best

signal from these measurements and ranking of surrounding cell sites. Once the session is

initiated, the handoff cell sites are chosen by the network. This is significant because the initial

cell site will be the best signal that the cellular phone measures, while the handoff cell sites will

be chosen by the network based on known neighbors to the selected cell site. In other words, the

location of a device is best estimated by the initial cell site versus the handoff cell site. Handoffs

are mapped on a case-by-case basis, which is determined by the needs of the investigation.

        FBI CAST's conclusions and opinions of the phone's location in this case do include the

analysis of the handoff data. On page 13 of the CAST Final Report, the defendant's phone uses

cell site with eNodeB and Sector 424199-15, which is consistent with the phone not being at the

defendant's residence. (See State's Exhibit S-2(b) to State's Supplemental Response Re: Expert

Disclosures) This conclusion would have been reached with or without drive test data, but was

further supported by the drive test data.

        AT&T call detail records include voice, data, and SMS (text message) connections. The

 The following is in part the basis for my opinion in this matter. This is not an
exhaustive literature review but is representative of the current understanding
of the effects of alcohol on the human body and intended to provide insight into
                      its effect on performance and behavior.


Ethanol is a central nervous system (CNS) depressant. Consumption of ethanol impairs the
brain in a dose-dependent manner (see Table 1) with impairment first appearing at blood
levels well below the per se limit of 0.08% for non-commercial vehicles (Tagawa et al, 2000;
Starkey and Charlton, 2014; Van Dyke and Fillmore, 2015; Charlton and Starkey, 2015;
Moskowitz et al, 2000; Hendler et al, 2013). The more ethanol consumed, the greater its
effects and the greater the impairment (Taylor et al, 2010). Ethanol impairs both cognition2
and psychomotor3 skills to a degree often unappreciated by the consumer (Verster and
Roth, 2012; Brick and Erickson, 2009).

Even low doses of alcohol commonly consumed during social drinking (1–3 drinks) produce
mild intoxication: euphoria, stimulation, disinhibition, talkativeness, loss of social fear,
relaxation, sedation, and produce an impact on cognition, attention, motor behavior, and
emotional processing (Dry et al, 2012; Bisby et al, 2010; Jaaskelainen 1999; Breitmeier et al,
2007; Kamboj 2013).

The quantitative determination of ethanol in postmortem specimens has become a
relatively simple analytical procedure and accurate, precise, and specific results are
possible (Jones and Pounder, 1998). However, interpreting postmortem BAC results and
drawing correct conclusions about antemortem levels and the person’s state of inebriation
and degree of behavioral impairment at the time of death is fraught with difficulties (Leikin
and Watson, 2003; Ziavrou et al, 2003; Wigmore, 2011). The condition of the body, the time
between death and autopsy, the environmental conditions (temperature and humidity), and
the nature of the specimen collected for analysis are important factors to consider. Under
some circumstances alcohol might be produced after death by microbial activity and
fermentation of glucose, which is a concern if the corpse has undergone decomposition.
Postmortem diffusion of alcohol from the stomach to central blood sampling sites is another
complicating factor if a person died shortly after a period of heavy drinking. Care is needed
to ensure that biological specimens are not contaminated with ethanol or other extraneous
solvents during any lifesaving treatment or in connection with external examination of the
body (Kugelberg and Jones, 2007).

In this case there is limited information available to me about the extent of the alcohol
consumption by Madison Mogen on the night of 11/12 – 11/13/22 other than she appeared
“intoxicated” upon returning home. The information suggests that the interval from the
time of last alcohol consumption by Mogen and the time of the attack was about 3 hours.
This interval would allow for complete absorption of alcohol (i.e. the post-absorptive stage)
from the stomach and minimize the confounding of postmortem redistribution from
stomach contents (Wigmore 2011).

When alcohol is absorbed into the blood it distributes to total body water not to any
particular tissue or organ. The source of the blood toxicology sample from Mogen was
noted to be “cavity blood” or extravascular blood and was reported as 0.282 g/100 ml.

        Regarding genome sequencing, Othram uses Whole Genome Shotgun Sequencing

(WGSS) as a method to recover useful DNA information from forensic evidence. However,

the genome without the biases of targeted sequencing methods or the limitations of microarrays,

which struggle with degraded or low-quantity forensic samples. Othram’s goal is to extract the

accelerate the investigative process. While this approach expedited the identification, it did not

change the inevitable outcome—the suspect would have been identified regardless.

already begun, but Othram was on track to reach the same conclusion. This is evident from the

    METHOD SUMMARY
    This paper illustrates measures that can be implemented to protect genomic data derived from
    reference testing of third-party individuals during a forensic genetic genealogy investigation.
    Privacy of the genetic data of these individuals is paramount and can be enhanced by offline

    party individuals and those of crime scene evidence or human remains. Additionally, SNP matching
    information are aggregated into bins. Instead of individual SNP marker states displayed as

        In response to the Defendant’s expert disclosures, and in addition to previously disclosed

follows:

        Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is genetic material contained in cells that may be utilized

for human identification. DNA analysis includes the utilization of Polymerase Chain Reaction

(PCR) to generate Short Tandem Repeats (STR) profiles. DNA may be present on an item in the

form of biological materials such as blood, semen, and saliva or may be left on an item when it is

worn (wearer DNA) or handled (touch or transfer DNA). DNA can be collected by swabbing an

item or by taking a cutting directly from an item. Reference DNA samples can be collected

directly from individuals. DNA can be used to identify individuals, link individuals to crime

scenes, link individuals to each other, link crime scenes, as well as exclude individuals from

DNA profiles that we obtain.

        Interpreting DNA data and making comparisons requires the development of DNA

profiles. There are four steps to developing a DNA profile:

            1. Extraction: add chemicals to break open all the cells to release the DNA.
            2. Quantification: amount of DNA is measured within each sample.
            3. Amplification: copy the specific locations of DNA using polymerase chain
               reaction.
            4. Developing a DNA profile: visualize the DNA profile, which looks like peaks on
               a graph.

        A single source DNA profile refers to DNA from only one person. A mixture refers to

DNA from two or more individuals. DNA can be deposited on or transferred to an item through

                                                                                          CR01-24-31665
transfer of body fluids to an item, wearing an item, and/or handling an item. Primary or direct

transfer refers to DNA transferring directly between individuals or between an individual and an

object. Indirect or secondary transfer refers to the transfer of DNA from one person or object to

another person or object through an intermediary.

        The amount of DNA transferred to an item varies and can be impacted by the nature of

the transfer. Transfer of a body fluid typically involves a higher amount of DNA transfer than

handling an item. Biological factors can influence the primary or direct transfer of DNA. This

can include the shedder status of the individual(s), personal hygiene and rate of perspiration. The

composition of the item can influence the primary or direct transfer of DNA. For example, the

transfer of DNA is more likely when the item is absorbent rather than non-absorbent. The length

of the contact can influence the primary or direct transfer of DNA. The longer a person is in

contact with an item the more likely DNA transfer will occur. The nature of contact can influence

the primary or direct transfer of DNA. A person who manipulates an item is more likely to

transfer DNA to the item than a person who casually touches an item. The persistence of DNA on

an item can also vary depending on several factors, including the amount of DNA initially

transferred, the composition of the item, exposure of the item post transfer to various

environmental conditions (heat, precipitation, UV radiation, etc.), and whether the item is

cleaned.

        All these factors can also influence the indirect or secondary transfer of DNA. In

addition, secondary transfer can be influenced by the time between the direct transfer and the

potential secondary transfer. Shorter time typically results in less loss of DNA prior to the

potential secondary transfer.

                                  Anticipated Rebuttal Testimony

        In response to the Defendant’s expert disclosures, and in addition to previously disclosed

testimony, the State may call Katie White (formerly Dace) as an expert witness to respond, rebut

and testify as follows:

        The Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) is a national collection of DNA profiles

maintained by the FBI. It contains convicted offender profiles, arrestee profiles, as well as

profiles obtained from evidence, and can be used to identify a suspect, generate potential leads,

or link crime scenes together. Upon entry, profiles are automatically searched against the

database and generate “hits” when a match occurs.

        In Idaho, individuals convicted of felonies and those placed on the sex offender registry

must be entered into CODIS. Idaho also allows for the entry of DNA profiles associated with

unidentified human remains or living people unable to identify themselves. Idaho also allows for

the entry of unidentified profiles generated from evidence associated with a crime, when that

profile is believed to be from the putative perpetrator.

        The profiles are entered by the reporting analyst and reviewed during technical review.

Idaho State Police Forensics Services is required to follow the NDIS Operational Procedures

Manual and abide by an MOU between the FBI and the Idaho State police. Each state also has

one CODIS Administrator who is responsible for ensuring only approved profiles are entered.

White is the State CODIS Administrator for Idaho.

        White agrees with the reporting analyst’s decision not to enter the unknown profiles from

Male B and Male D into CODIS because those DNA samples were not eligible for entry into

CODIS. DNA profiles believed to be attributable to the putative perpetrator are eligible for entry

into CODIS. DNA profiles attributable to victims, nonprobative individuals, and items without an

established link to the crime/crime scene are not allowed to be entered.

        The laboratory must justify entry of every profile put into CODIS and many factors can

influence eligibility. For a large crime scene, not all locations within the scene are always weighted

equally in probative value. It is very common to locate nonprobative biological stains within

residences due to everyday activities such as consensual sexual activity, small injuries, and lack of

cleanliness. Evidence located closer to a point of entry, a disturbed area, or a victim will typically be

considered of higher probative value than other areas.

        For this case, a knife sheath next to one of the victims produced an unknown male profile.

The victims were believed to have been stabbed to death and the sheath’s proximity to the victim’s

body provided the strongest link to a potential perpetrator. The residence where the crime occurred

is heavily trafficked by college students and the location of the blood spot on the underside of a

handrail (Male B) does not in itself indicate a link to the crime. A gardening style glove located

outside the residence in a publicly accessible area produced an additional unknown male profile

(Male D). No DNA attributable to the victims was located on this item and a direct link to the crime

could not be established. Had DNA from one or more of the victims been located on this item, it

likely would have been CODIS eligible.

        At the time CODIS eligibility was determined, Male DNA profiles A, B, and D were

unidentified, and it was not possible for lab staff to know who would ultimately be linked to any one

item. The CODIS eligibility determination was strictly made based on an evaluation of the probative

value of each item.
