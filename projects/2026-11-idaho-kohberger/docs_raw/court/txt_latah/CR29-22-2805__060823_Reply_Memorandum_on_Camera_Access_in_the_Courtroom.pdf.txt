        The State, Mr. Kohberger, and Intervenors share many views on audio/visual coverage of

this case. All agree that the Court ultimately has discretion to decide whether to allow audio/visual

coverage. Nobody seems to dispute that most members of the impacted communities would be

unable to observe the proceedings with their own eyes and ears absent audio/visual coverage or

that there would be many benefits for the public and the criminal justice system if impacted

members of the community could receive contemporaneous, complete, and objective evidence about

this case as it is presented in the courtroom. Nonetheless, the State flags some concerns about

audio/visual coverage while largely deferring to the Court and Mr. Kohberger more forcefully objects.

        Idaho Court Administrative Rule 45 largely addresses the concerns that the State and

Mr. Kohberger raise. Some witnesses may be acutely affected by audio/visual coverage, but those

concerns can be addressed as they come up and on an individualized basis. Idaho Ct. Admin. R. 45(d).

        The jury should hear only the evidence or arguments submitted to them, but Rule 45 regulates

audio/visual coverage such that effectively only those aspects of the proceedings open to the public in

the gallery are broadcasted. Intervenors Supplemental Memorandum on Camera Access in the

Courtroom (“Opening Mem.”) at pp. 6–7. Regardless of whether the Court allows audio/visual

coverage, there is going to be large amounts of publicity surrounding this case. There is no greater risk

that potential jurors (for pre-trial proceedings) or jurors (for trial) will be unfairly biased if there is

audio/visual coverage, as any information shared in open court that is not broadcasted may still be

published by reporters who attend the proceedings. For that reason, the Court will certainly instruct the

jurors not to watch news coverage regardless of whether it allows audio/visual coverage.

        Audio/visual coverage will not create a distraction in the courtroom. As Judge Boyce noted in

Lori Vallow’s case: “[T]he Court affirms that there is no indication that any orders relating to the

conduct of the media during hearings in this case, or the companion case, Fremont County Case

No. CR22-21-1623, State of Idaho vs. Chad Guy Daybell, have been violated. The Court has likewise

witnessed no misconduct on any part of the media during hearings in theses cases. The presence of

media during the hearings has in no way interrupted those proceedings, and attending media have been

respectful and professional.” State’s Brief on Video and Photographic Coverage, Ex. at p. 3. Those

observations are unsurprising and consistent with Rule 45’s carefully crafted regulations of the media’s

conduct. Opening Mem. at pp. 7–8.

        Finally, Intervenors appreciate Mr. Kohberger’s concerns about how certain images have been

used on social media. Prohibiting audio/visual coverage will not eliminate the concern though, as those

in the courtroom could publish similar statements about their observations of Mr. Kohberger’s

appearance and demeanor. To address Mr. Kohberger’s concerns, while still allowing impacted

community members to view the proceedings, the Court could regulate how cameras capture Mr.

Kohberger, by for example allowing only wider shots or limiting how close the camera can zoom.

What is more, a few selective examples are not reason to deprive all the members of the impacted

communities the opportunity to observe the proceedings with their own eyes and ears. And again, once

a jury is empaneled, the Court will certainly instruct them not to consume any information related to

the case outside of what is presented to them in the courtroom.

        Intervenors continue to request that the Court allow audio/visual coverage consistent with

Idaho Court Administrative Rule 45.
